# Personal reflection — NALI 2026

**Wong Wen Hao · AI Japanese Learning Assistant**

## What I learned

The interview changed how I evaluate educational AI. A system should do more than return an answer: its feedback should help a learner recognise and correct a mistake. In pronunciation practice, a quick response may support independent learning, but an inaccurate assessment could reinforce the wrong pronunciation. I would therefore judge the system by the usefulness and accuracy of its feedback, alongside its speed.

This connected TIS concepts to a complete experience. Learner input, network access, AI processing and the feedback interface depend on one another. If a connection fails or the feedback is confusing, the student experiences a problem with the whole system, even when an individual component is working.

The discussion also helped me separate personalisation from empathy. Matching practice to a student's level may be useful, but it does not necessarily explain why a student is frustrated or losing confidence. Teachers still have an important role in checking feedback and deciding how to support a learner.

## Challenges and how I approached them

An important challenge was describing the project accurately while distinguishing current functionality from future plans. For example, local AI infrastructure was discussed as a possible privacy-related alternative. Presenting that as an implemented feature would exaggerate the evidence. I kept that distinction in my reflection and supporting explanation.

Producing a coherent group video also involved recordings with different framing and audio quality. The edit used separate recorder audio where it was available and embedded camera audio where it was not. Portrait footage needed landscape crops, which improved consistency but necessarily removed some visual context. This showed me that editing cannot fully repair every recording decision.

Reviewing the speaker labels exposed another practical issue: a list of names does not reliably establish where each person stands in a shot. The labels were corrected after the identities were clarified against a screenshot. Next time, I would record each interviewee's name and position together with a reference frame before leaving the booth.

## Skills developed

The work practised interviewing, technical summarisation and communication through video. I had to connect the poster, the interview answers and my reflection instead of treating them as separate pieces of information. Reviewing scripts and figures also helped me decide when a visual explanation adds value and when it distracts from the speaker.

Using AI tools required review rather than simply accepting their output. Scripts needed factual checks, generated avatar scenes needed to fit the spoken explanation, and automatic subtitles needed corrections. The final responsibility for the submitted work remains with the group.

## What I would improve next time

I would agree a recording checklist before the visit: landscape framing, a short audio test, a visible poster photograph, confirmed interviewee details and consistent file names. I would also reserve time for a complete listening review of the subtitles before submission.

For a future educational application, I would test feedback against examples checked by a lecturer, observe whether students understand the corrections, and estimate the running cost under realistic use. I would also document what student data is collected, why it is needed and who can access it. These checks would make a stronger case for classroom use than a successful demonstration alone.

## Relevance to my studies and future work

The visit made the course concepts more concrete because the project team had to deal with actual learners, teaching time and resource limits. It increased my interest in building systems whose technical behaviour supports a clear human purpose. In future coursework, I want to evaluate functionality together with feedback quality, usability and the responsibilities of the people operating the system.
